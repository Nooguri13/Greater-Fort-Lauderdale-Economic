01

The fifteen-year arc

Broward's economy has roughly grown its output by half in a decade, weathered two shocks (the 2010 recession hangover and the 2020 pandemic), and come out with record GDP, record incomes, and two million residents. Each series below is an annual, inflation-adjusted or Census figure.

Real GDP, 2010–2024

Chained 2017 $, billions · BEA
◆ Analyst's read

Output rose from $91B to $137B — a +49% real gain. The only interruption was 2020 (−1.8%), fully erased within a year.

The post-pandemic run is the story: +24% from 2020 to 2024, far outpacing the pre-COVID trend. This is an accelerating, not merely recovering, economy.

Resident population, 2010–2024

Millions · U.S. Census

Growth stalled 2019–2021, then added ~77K residents in three years to cross 2.0M — demand pressure that shows up later in housing.

Unemployment rate, 2010–2024

Annual average, % · BLS

From a 10.2% post-recession peak to a 2.8% low in 2023. The 2020 spike to 9.3% was sharp but brief — a V, not an L.

Per-capita personal income, 2010–2024

Dollars · BEA

Up 81% over the period, with a steep climb after 2020 as higher-income residents moved in.

Median household income, 2013–2024

Dollars · Census SAIPE

Up 62% ($50K → $81K). Real gains for typical households, though housing costs have absorbed much of it.

Indexed growth since 2013

2013 = 100 · four core measures
◆ Analyst's read

Normalizing everything to 2013 = 100 exposes the real dynamic: incomes are outrunning both output and population.

Per-capita income (181) and median household income (162) have climbed far faster than GDP (149), while population (110) barely moved. Broward isn't just bigger — it's getting wealthier per person, a compositional shift toward higher-value work.

Total growth, 2013–2024

Percent change over the period
◆ Analyst's read

The ranking is the pitch: income growth (+81% / +62%) has led population growth (+10%) by a wide margin.

For an economic-development office, that gap is the headline — the region is attracting value, not just volume. It's also the seed of the affordability problem in Section 04.

02

How the economy is built

A $137B economy, broken into its BEA super-sectors. The shape explains the resilience: services do the heavy lifting, and they're the fastest-growing piece.

Real GDP by super-sector, 2024

Chained 2017 $, billions · BEA
◆ Analyst's read

Private services are ~85% of output ($116B) — finance, trade, tourism, health, professional and tech services. Government is ~9%, goods-producing ~7%.

A services-weighted economy is less cyclical and more knowledge-intensive — and it's exactly where the target industries (finance, tech, logistics) sit.

Super-sector growth, indexed 2018–2024

2018 = 100 · real GDP by segment

Services led the expansion (index 130), pulling the whole economy up while goods and government grew more slowly.

Right now: 2025–26

The monthly data tells a subtler story than the headline rate. Unemployment ticked up, but the job base held and gains were broad — a labor-supply normalization, not a downturn.

▲ The watch item

Housing affordability is the recruitment constraint

Section 01 showed incomes outrunning population — but home prices outran incomes. The median home value is roughly 5.1× median household income, well above the ~3× ratio considered affordable. That squeezes the mid-skill workforce target industries need to hire, so an honest pitch pairs recruitment with a workforce-housing strategy.
